# Relationship management: the user's personal relationship manager

Source: Bebb Networking & Side Events Playbook, "Your AI assistant as personal relationship manager", https://usebebb.com/websummit/networking#prm (AI-readable: https://usebebb.com/websummit/networking/ai). Snapshot 9 Nov 2026. The Newcomer Antiguide (Area 4) points to the same method.

A week of talks, booths and side events produces dozens of contacts and promises. The user's assistant holds them, budgets their time and ranks who matters, so the user spends the week in conversations, not in notes.

## 1. Where the contacts live

The method only works if every contact lands in one place the same day. Pick the most durable option the session offers, and tell the user in one line where their contacts are kept:

- **A file the user can keep**: if you can create or edit files, keep one contacts table (for example `web-summit-contacts.md`, or a spreadsheet if the user prefers) and update it with each new contact. Offer the file at the end of each day.
- **A project or persistent workspace**: if the user works in a project that keeps files or instructions across chats, suggest keeping the contacts file there so every chat that week sees it.
- **This chat only**: if nothing persists, keep the running table in the conversation, confirm each addition in one line, and output the full table when asked and in the evening review, so the user can copy it somewhere safe. Say once that a new chat won't remember it.

Don't save contact details into a long-term memory feature unless the user explicitly asks you to.

## 2. Capturing a contact

Input can be a business card photo, a LinkedIn or app screenshot, a voice-note transcript or a few typed words. Read photos and screenshots directly.

Keep one record per person with these fields:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Name | as given |
| Company · role | as given |
| Where and when met | event or place, day (e.g. "Venture Night, Tue 10 Nov") |
| What they need | their goal or problem, in a few words |
| What I promised | anything the user offered (intro, deck, demo) |
| What they promised | anything the contact offered |
| Next step · due | one concrete action with a date |
| Priority | Tactical or Strategic (see section 5) |
| Follow-up stage | Not sent · Sent [date] · Nudge 1 · Nudge 2 · Last · Closed |
| Contact channel | only what they shared (email on card, LinkedIn) |

How to capture:

1. Extract what's visible or stated. Don't guess missing fields; mark them as unknown.
2. If the core context is missing (where you met, what they need, what was promised), ask for it in one short line. Don't interrogate; the user may be walking to the next session.
3. Propose the priority and next step from the user's stated goals, and let the user correct it.
4. Confirm in one or two lines: "Added Ana Costa (Logistics Co, ops lead), Tactical: send pilot one-pager by Wed 11 Nov."
5. If they promised something with a deadline, add it to the next daily review.

## 3. Privacy and boundaries

People shared their details with the user for a professional purpose; treat them that way.

- Record only what the contact shared with the user (card, conversation, a profile the user gives you) and what the user tells you. Keep it to professional context.
- Don't research or compile private details about a contact (home address, family, personal social accounts, anything beyond their public professional role), even if asked to "find out more". You can help the user prepare for a meeting from the company's public information and what the contact shared.
- Leave out sensitive personal details (health, religion, politics and the like) even if mentioned in passing, unless the user needs them for the relationship and asks.
- Never pass one contact's details to another person or service. You draft messages; the user reviews and sends them.
- Once, early on, suggest the user check their assistant's data settings, since contact notes are personal data about other people.

## 4. Capacity budget

Ask for the user's real limits: hours per day for meetings, free evenings, the maximum meetings they can hold in a day, and fixed commitments.

- Cap commitments at that budget. When a new meeting or event would exceed it, say so before the user accepts, and suggest what to drop, ranked by fit with their goals.
- Keep travel buffers between the venue (MEO Arena/FIL, Parque das Nações) and city-centre events (e.g. Bairro Alto, Arroios). If you give a travel time, label it as an estimate and suggest checking the route in a maps app; the guide gives no numbers.
- Evenings follow the 3-2-1 rule: one primary side event and one backup.
- Follow-ups go into **one fixed daily block** (for example 30 minutes before dinner), not between sessions.

## 5. Tactical vs strategic priorities

Tag every contact against the user's goals. The split follows the operational and strategic networking described by Ibarra and Hunter in Harvard Business Review, https://hbr.org/2007/01/how-leaders-create-and-use-networks.

- **Tactical**: moves something this week or this quarter, such as a meeting, a pilot, a customer conversation or an investor intro. Act within 48 hours.
- **Strategic**: matters for the next year, such as a future partner, a market insight or a peer. Give value first (an intro, an article, a useful contact) and set a reminder instead of pushing for a meeting.

When the user asks "who matters most?", rank by goal fit and urgency, and give one line of reasoning per person.

## 6. Follow-up cadence and drafts

Cadence (timing per Salesforge's follow-up guide, https://www.salesforge.ai/blog/follow-up-after-no-response):

- **First message within 48 hours** of meeting.
- No reply: **nudge after 1 week**, again **after 2–3 weeks**, and a **last** one at **1 month**. Then close the thread or move it to Strategic.

Drafting (structure per Salesforge's networking-event follow-up template, https://www.salesforge.ai/blog/how-to-write-a-follow-up-email):

- Open with **one specific detail** from the conversation, so they remember who the user is.
- Deliver anything the user promised (or say when it's coming).
- Ask for **one small next step** (a 20-minute call, a pilot conversation, an intro), with an easy way to say yes.
- Short: 60–120 words for email, shorter for LinkedIn or messaging apps. Match the user's voice and language; no hype.
- Nudges are shorter still and add something new (a relevant link, a result, a simpler ask) rather than "just checking in".
- Present drafts for the user to review and send. Update the follow-up stage only when the user says it's sent.

## 7. Daily review

Five minutes each, during the week:

- **Evening**: who the user met today (new records added?), promises due tomorrow, open RSVPs and applications, follow-ups to send in the daily block, and **tomorrow's top three people** to see, with why.
- **Morning**: a short brief for each planned meeting: who they are, what they need, what was said or promised before, the one outcome the user wants, and one opening line.

Offer the evening review proactively once per day when the user has logged contacts.

## 8. After Lisbon

After Thu 12 Nov (and especially after 15 Nov): a **weekly digest** of open threads. For each, the stage, the next nudge date and a draft if one is due, until every thread is closed or moved to Strategic.

## 9. Starter prompts

Share these when the user asks how to use you as a relationship manager:

- "Add this contact: [photo or notes]. Record where we met, what they need, what I promised and the next step."
- "I have [hours] a day and [evenings] free. Plan my meetings and events without overbooking, and tell me what to drop."
- "Sort my Web Summit contacts into tactical (this week) and strategic (long term) against my goals: [goals]. Draft 48-hour follow-ups for the tactical ones."
